Run your hand over the back of your baby's head at two or three months, and you might find it: a flat patch, maybe more on one side, maybe with one ear looking slightly forward of the other. Cue the spiral of guilt and late-night searching. Take a breath first: flat spots became dramatically more common for the best possible reason — back sleeping, which the AAP recommends because it sharply reduced SIDS deaths. The trade-off is positional plagiocephaly, which is cosmetic in the vast majority of cases, responds well to simple habits, and does not affect brain development. Here's the practical playbook.

> 📌 **Key Takeaway:** Positional flat spots come from time spent with the head resting in one position — and according to the AAP, they don't affect brain growth or development. Prevention and early treatment are the same three habits: supervised tummy time every day from the start, alternating head position and feeding sides, and cutting down "container time" in swings, seats and carriers. Always keep back sleeping for every sleep — a flat spot is never a reason to change safe sleep. Mention flattening to your pediatrician at checkups; they'll distinguish it from rarer causes and advise if more is needed.

## Why Flat Spots Happen

A young baby's skull is soft and fast-growing — that's what makes birth possible and brain growth easy. It also means sustained pressure on one spot can flatten it, the way a ripe peach flattens where it rests on the counter.

The common version is **positional plagiocephaly** (flattening on one side, sometimes pushing the ear or forehead slightly forward on that side) or **brachycephaly** (flattening across the whole back). Contributors include back sleeping, lots of time in car seats/swings/bouncers while awake, prematurity (softer skulls, more NICU time on the back), multiples (tighter quarters in the womb), and — importantly — **torticollis**, a tight neck muscle that makes a baby prefer looking one way. Torticollis and flat spots feed each other, which is why the "always looks left" baby deserves a pediatrician conversation, not just repositioning.

What flat spots are *not*: a sign of anything wrong with the brain. The AAP is direct on this — positional skull flattening is a shape issue, not a development issue.

> ⚠️ **Safe sleep is non-negotiable:** every sleep on the back, on a firm flat surface, no positioners, wedges or special pillows — the FDA and AAP warn that infant head-shaping pillows are unnecessary and have been linked to suffocation risk. Flat head prevention happens during *awake* time, never by changing how your baby sleeps. See our [safe sleep guide](/blog/baby-sleep-safety-sids-prevention/) for the full rules.

## The Prevention Playbook (Which Is Also the Treatment)

For most babies, the fix is redistributing pressure across the day. Repositioning works best started early — the AAP notes improvement is most likely while the skull is growing fastest, in the first months.

## When More Than Repositioning Is Discussed

**Physical therapy** is the usual next step when torticollis is in the picture — a therapist teaches stretches and positioning that free up the neck, which in turn takes pressure off the flat side. It's low-drama and effective, and pediatricians refer for it readily.

**Helmet therapy** (a custom molded orthosis worn most of the day for a few months) enters the conversation mainly for *moderate-to-severe* flattening that hasn't improved with repositioning by around 5–6 months. The honest evidence summary: the AAP notes that mild flattening generally improves without helmets, that helmets can help in more severe cases within the right age window, and that specialists disagree at the margins. It's a genuinely case-by-case decision about severity, age and family preference — made with your pediatrician or a specialist, not from an article, and certainly not from a helmet company's marketing.

**The rare thing your pediatrician is ruling out** is craniosynostosis — skull bones fusing early, which causes unusual head shapes that can *look* superficially similar but need specialist care. This is one strong reason to bring any flattening to a checkup rather than self-diagnosing: distinguishing positional flattening from rarer causes is exactly what those head-shape checks at well visits are for. It's also why unusual ridges along the skull, a head that seems to grow oddly in circumference, or flattening that worsens despite good habits deserve an appointment rather than watching.

## Common Questions About Flat Head Syndrome

### Will my baby's flat head fix itself?

Mild positional flattening usually improves substantially on its own as your baby gains head control, spends more awake time upright, and starts rolling and sitting — especially when repositioning habits and daily tummy time start early. The AAP notes most mild cases don't need anything beyond these measures. Moderate or severe flattening that isn't improving by around 5–6 months deserves a pediatrician conversation about next steps.

### Does flat head syndrome affect brain development?

No — according to the AAP, positional plagiocephaly is a head-shape issue, not a brain issue. It doesn't affect brain growth, intelligence or development. When flattening is linked to torticollis (a tight neck muscle), the torticollis itself is treated so movement develops normally, but the flat spot itself is cosmetic in the vast majority of cases.

### Should I use a pillow or sleep positioner to prevent flat head?

No. The FDA and AAP warn against infant head-shaping pillows, wedges and positioners — they're unnecessary and have been associated with suffocation deaths. Babies should sleep on their backs on a firm, flat surface with nothing else in the sleep space, every sleep. All flat-head prevention happens during awake time: tummy time, upright holding, alternating positions, less container time.

### When would my baby need a helmet?

Helmets are typically discussed for moderate-to-severe flattening that hasn't improved with repositioning and tummy time, usually evaluated around 5–6 months while skull growth is still fast. Mild cases generally don't benefit enough to justify one, per AAP guidance. Whether a helmet makes sense involves measured severity, age, torticollis treatment, and family preference — a decision to make with your pediatrician or a craniofacial specialist.

### How do I know if it's torticollis?

Clues include a baby who always looks or tilts to the same side, dislikes turning the head the other way, feeds noticeably better on one side, or develops flattening on one spot despite repositioning efforts. Torticollis is common, and pediatricians check neck movement at well visits — raise it if you've noticed a preference. Treatment is usually simple stretching and positioning, often with a physical therapist's guidance.

## The Bottom Line

- **Flat spots are common, cosmetic in most cases, and don't affect the brain** — they're a side effect of back sleeping, which stays non-negotiable
- **The playbook is awake-time pressure relief:** daily tummy time, alternating head position and feeding sides, upright holding, less container time
- **Start habits early** — repositioning works best in the first months while the skull grows fastest
- **A head-turn preference is a torticollis clue** — raise it at a checkup; stretches solve most of it
- **Helmets are a case-by-case call for moderate-to-severe cases around 5–6 months** — and pillows and positioners are never the answer

If you've just found a flat patch, you haven't broken your baby — you've been following safe sleep advice, and this is the manageable trade that came with it. Start the habits today, mention it at the next well visit, and let your pediatrician track the shape. This article is general education; your baby's doctor is the one who can assess your actual baby's head.
