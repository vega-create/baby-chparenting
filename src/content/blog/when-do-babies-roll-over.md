Rolling over is one of your baby's first big physical milestones — the moment they go from staying wherever you put them to actually moving their body through space. It's exciting to witness, but it also raises questions: when should it happen, how can you encourage it, and what does it mean for safe sleep? This guide covers the typical rolling timeline, how to help your baby build the strength for it, the important sleep-safety change it triggers, and when a delay is worth mentioning to your pediatrician.

> 📌 **Key Takeaway:** According to the CDC and AAP, most babies roll from tummy to back around 4 months and back to tummy by about 6 months, though the range is wide and normal. Once your baby can roll, a key safety rule changes: you still place them on their back to sleep, but you no longer need to reposition a baby who rolls on their own.

## The Rolling Timeline

Tummy-to-back often comes first because it's a bit easier — sometimes it even happens by accident when a baby pushes up during tummy time and tips over. Back-to-tummy takes more strength and coordination, so it usually comes a little later. Some babies do them in the opposite order or skip ahead, which is also fine.

## How to Encourage Rolling

The single best thing you can do is **tummy time** — it builds the neck, shoulder, arm, and core strength rolling requires. Beyond that:

- ✅ **Do daily [tummy time](/blog/tummy-time-complete-guide/)** — starting from the newborn days, building up as they grow
- ✅ **Use toys as motivation** — place a favorite toy just out of reach to their side
- ✅ **Give floor freedom** — lots of supervised time on a safe, flat surface (not confined in seats and swings)
- ✅ **Get down and play** — babies love mirroring and interacting face to face
- ✅ **Gently demonstrate** — you can help guide the motion so they feel it

Babies who spend lots of time in containers (bouncers, swings, car seats beyond travel) get less chance to practice. Free floor play is where motor skills develop.

## The Big Safety Change When Baby Rolls

This is important. Once your baby can roll both ways on their own:

> ⚠️ **Safe sleep update:** Continue to **always place your baby on their back to sleep** — that never changes. But once your baby can roll both ways independently, you don't need to reposition them if they roll onto their tummy during sleep. Also, this is the time to **stop swaddling** — a swaddled baby who rolls to their tummy can't push up, which is dangerous. Transition out of the swaddle as soon as your baby shows signs of rolling.

Also double-check the crib is bare — no loose blankets, pillows, or bumpers — since a mobile baby moves around more. Our [baby-proofing guide](/blog/baby-proofing-home-checklist/) covers the broader safety picture as your baby becomes mobile.

## When to Check With Your Pediatrician

Milestones have a wide normal range, so try not to compare your baby to others. That said, mention it at a check-up if:

- Your baby **isn't rolling either way by around 6 months**
- Your baby **rolled and then stopped** doing it entirely
- You notice your baby **strongly favoring one side** or seems stiff or floppy
- There are **other developmental concerns** alongside the delay

Premature babies should be assessed on their **adjusted age** (from the due date), which often explains a "delay." A delay in one milestone alone is usually not a concern, but your pediatrician is the right person to reassure you or take a closer look.

## Common Questions About Rolling Over

### At what age do babies roll over?

Most babies roll from tummy to back around 4 months and from back to tummy by about 5-6 months, with rolling both ways comfortably by around 6 months. The normal range is wide, so some babies roll earlier and others later. Tummy time helps build the strength needed for this milestone.

### Which way do babies roll first?

Most babies roll from tummy to back first, often around 4 months, because it takes a little less strength and sometimes happens almost by accident during tummy time. Back-to-tummy usually comes a bit later, around 5-6 months, as it requires more core and arm strength. Some babies do it in the opposite order, which is also normal.

### Should I still swaddle once my baby starts rolling?

No — stop swaddling as soon as your baby shows signs of rolling. A swaddled baby who rolls onto their tummy can't use their arms to push up, which is a safety risk. Transition to a sleep sack or wearable blanket that leaves the arms free, so your baby can move safely if they roll during sleep.

### Do I need to reposition my baby if they roll over in their sleep?

Once your baby can roll both ways on their own, you don't need to reposition them if they roll onto their tummy during sleep — but you should still always place them on their back to start. Keep the crib bare and safe. If your baby can't yet roll both ways, gently return them to their back.

### What if my baby isn't rolling by 6 months?

If your baby isn't rolling either way by around 6 months, mention it at your next check-up so your pediatrician can take a look. A single delayed milestone is often not a concern, especially for premature babies assessed on their adjusted age. Your pediatrician can reassure you or evaluate further if needed.

## The Bottom Line

Rolling over is your baby's first taste of independent movement. Keep these principles in mind:

- **Tummy to back around 4 months**, back to tummy by ~6 months (wide normal range)
- **Tummy time is the key** to building rolling strength
- **Free floor play** beats time in containers for motor development
- **Stop swaddling and update sleep safety** once your baby can roll both ways
- **Check with your pediatrician** if there's no rolling by ~6 months or a loss of the skill

Celebrate this milestone — it's the start of your baby's mobile adventures (and your cue to keep an even closer eye on where you set them down). Every baby rolls in their own time, so cheer yours on and enjoy the ride.
