The crib is the last piece of baby furniture that works entirely in your favor. Your child is contained, the room is safe by default, and bedtime ends when you walk out. A toddler bed hands all of that back to a two-year-old with no impulse control and a new sense of possibility. So the honest advice is: don't rush it, and when you do switch, childproof the room before you move the furniture. Here's when the switch is actually necessary and how to make it as short as possible.

> 📌 **Key Takeaway:** There's no developmental reason to move to a bed early — the usual triggers are climbing out, outgrowing the crib's height limit, or needing the crib for a new baby. Once a child is climbing out, the crib has become the less safe option and the switch is on. Before the bed arrives, the room itself becomes the sleep space, so safe-sleep and home-safety thinking has to cover the whole room: anchored furniture, covered outlets, blind cords out of reach, a door strategy. Expect roughly two weeks of boundary testing. If your child has a medical condition, a sleep disorder, or climbs out repeatedly despite a lowered mattress, ask your pediatrician for advice specific to them. This is general information.

## The Three Reasons to Switch

**1. They're climbing out.** This is the only urgent one. A fall from the top rail is a worse risk than a toddler who can wander, so once climbing starts you have two options: lower the mattress to its lowest setting and remove anything they're using as a step, or move to a bed. If they're still getting out after that, move to the bed.

**2. They've outgrown it.** Cribs have manufacturer height limits — commonly around 35 inches / 90 cm, but check your own model. Chest-height rails mean the containment has stopped working.

**3. You need the crib.** A new baby arriving is a legitimate reason, with one piece of timing advice: make the move six to eight weeks *before* the baby comes, so the older child doesn't experience it as eviction. Our [baby-proofing checklist](/blog/baby-proofing-home-checklist/) is worth a fresh pass at the same time.

What's not a reason: age alone, potty training, a sibling who moved earlier, or a crib that's begun to feel babyish to you. Many children stay happily in a crib until three, and a child who can't yet understand "stay in bed" will mostly demonstrate that they can't.

## Childproof the Room Before You Move the Furniture

This is the part people do second and should do first. The moment the crib goes, the whole room is the sleep space.

The "could they safely be alone in here for an hour?" test is the right standard, because at some point they will be.

## The Two-Week Plan

**Days 1–3: build the association.** The bed arrives, with their own bedding and ideally a say in choosing it. Naps in it first if naps still happen. Read stories sitting on it. Keep the entire rest of the [bedtime routine](/blog/toddler-bedtime-routine/) exactly as it was — same bath, same books, same songs, same order. One change at a time.

**Days 4–10: hold the boundary, boringly.** They will get out. The script that works is the dull one: walk them back, one short sentence ("it's sleep time, back to bed"), tuck in, leave. No negotiation, no second story, no lift into your bed, no visible exasperation. Twenty repeats on night one is normal and not a sign of failure — it's the number of repeats it takes for the boundary to look real.

**Days 11–14: expect the test.** Just as it settles, many toddlers try once more. Same response. Consistency between the two of you matters more than the technique: a child who learns that one parent's boundary is softer will reliably find that parent.

Things that make it easier:

- **A visible rule.** "Stay in bed until the light turns green" works for a surprising number of two- and three-year-olds. Toddler clocks are one of the few sleep gadgets that earn their money.
- **A pre-emptive answer to the stalling.** Water by the bed, last toilet trip in the routine, one more hug built in before you leave. Removes the excuses in advance.
- **Protecting daytime sleep.** A toddler who drops their nap mid-transition will be too wired at bedtime to cooperate. Our [dropping the morning nap guide](/blog/dropping-the-morning-nap/) covers the schedule side.
- **Not stacking changes.** Potty training, a new sibling, starting daycare and a new bed in the same fortnight is too much. Space them.

If it coincides with the [2-year sleep regression](/blog/two-year-sleep-regression/), be patient with yourself — those two together are genuinely hard, and it's a phase rather than a plateau.

> ⚠️ **Worth a call to your pediatrician if:** your child repeatedly climbs or falls from the bed in a way you can't make safe; they're waking screaming and inconsolable, or with breathing pauses, loud snoring or gasping; sleep has deteriorated dramatically for more than a few weeks; or they're having frequent night-time injuries. Also talk to your pediatrician before the switch if your child has a seizure disorder, developmental differences, or a history of sleepwalking, since the room-safety plan may need to go further.

## What If You've Already Switched and It's Chaos?

Going back to the crib is allowed, provided they aren't climbing out of it. It isn't a failure; it's recognizing they weren't ready. Wait a couple of months and try again — the second attempt is often dramatically easier because comprehension has caught up.

If going back isn't an option — climbing, or the crib is in use — then the fix is almost always boundary consistency plus a gate, not a new technique. Tighten the routine, make the response boring and identical every time, and get both parents saying exactly the same sentence.

## Common Questions About the Crib-to-Bed Switch

### When should you move a toddler from crib to bed?

When one of three things happens: they're climbing out, they've reached the crib's height limit (often around 35 inches, though check your model), or you need the crib for a new baby. Age alone isn't a reason, and many children stay happily in a crib until around three. Waiting until they can understand and follow "stay in bed" generally makes the transition much shorter.

### My toddler keeps climbing out of the crib — what do I do?

First, lower the mattress to its lowest setting and remove anything nearby that works as a step, including bumpers, large toys and sleep positioners. Dress them in a sleep sack if they're still in one, which makes climbing harder. If they keep getting out despite all that, the crib has become the less safe option and it's time for a low bed with the room fully childproofed.

### How do I keep my toddler in bed after the switch?

Consistency, delivered boringly. Walk them back, say one short sentence, tuck them in, leave — every single time, with no negotiation and no extra stories. Expect many repeats on the first nights. Remove the stalling excuses in advance with water by the bed and a last toilet trip, consider a toddler clock as a visible rule, and make sure both parents use the identical response.

### Should I use a bed rail or a gate?

Often both. A rail helps if you've gone straight to a full-size bed; a floor-level toddler bed may not need one, though a rug beside it is sensible. A gate at the bedroom door or the top of the stairs keeps a wandering toddler within a safe area. A lock on the bedroom door is not a safe option. Whatever you choose, decide before the first night rather than improvising at 2 a.m.

### Can I put my toddler back in the crib if the bed isn't working?

Yes, as long as they aren't climbing out of it — in that case the crib is no longer the safer choice. Reverting isn't a setback; it's acknowledging they weren't developmentally ready to stay in a bed. Waiting a couple of months and trying again usually goes far more smoothly, because understanding of the rule has caught up with their physical ability to break it.

## The Bottom Line

- **Climbing out, outgrowing the height limit, or needing the crib** are the reasons — not age
- **Later is usually easier**, because the switch depends on understanding "stay in bed"
- **Childproof the whole room first**: anchor furniture, secure windows and cords, plan the door
- **Keep every other part of the routine identical** and change one thing at a time
- **Hold the boundary boringly** — same sentence, same response, both parents
- **Two weeks is a realistic timeline**, with a final test right at the end

And if you're reading this with a crib still in the room and no climbing in sight: enjoy it. There is no prize for being early.
