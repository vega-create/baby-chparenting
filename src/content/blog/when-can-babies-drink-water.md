It's 34°C, your four-month-old is red-cheeked and grumpy, and someone at the park says you should give the baby a bottle of water. It sounds like the obviously caring thing to do. It is, in fact, the one piece of hot-weather advice that pediatric guidance specifically warns against — and the reason is worth understanding, because it isn't fussiness. Here's what babies actually need to drink at each age, how much water is appropriate once they start, and why "a little water can't hurt" isn't true for a newborn.

> 📌 **Key Takeaway:** For roughly the first six months, breast milk or formula supplies all the fluid a healthy baby needs, including in hot weather — extra water displaces the milk that carries their calories and can dilute the sodium in their blood, which matters because babies' kidneys are still immature. CDC infant nutrition guidance introduces small amounts of plain water from around 6 months, alongside solids, and toddler guidance describes water as a main drink from 12 months. If your baby seems dehydrated or you're worried about fluid in illness or heat, call your pediatrician rather than adding water on your own — oral rehydration is a medical decision, not a home one. This is general information, not medical advice.

## Why Not Before Six Months

Two reasons, and they stack.

**Displacement.** A newborn's stomach is tiny. Every ounce of water is an ounce of breast milk or formula they don't drink — and milk is where all the calories, protein, fat and micronutrients are. Repeatedly topping up with water can mean less weight gain, not better hydration.

**Dilution.** This is the serious one. Infant kidneys can't excrete excess water efficiently yet, so a large volume of plain water can lower the concentration of sodium in the blood. Doctors call this hyponatremia or water intoxication, and in babies it can cause irritability, drowsiness, low body temperature, swelling, and in severe cases seizures. It is uncommon, but it is also preventable, which is exactly why the guidance is as firm as it is.

The same logic applies to over-diluting formula to stretch a tin. If money or supply is the reason you're considering it, talk to your pediatrician or a local WIC office — this is a situation with better answers than dilution. The 6–12 month range is where parents most want a number, and honestly the number is small: a few sips from an open or straw cup at mealtimes, not a bottle of water between feeds. The purpose at this stage is as much about learning to drink from a cup as it is about hydration. Milk still does the heavy lifting — our [how much milk by age guide](/blog/how-much-milk-by-age/) has those volumes.

## Starting Water With Solids

Around six months, when your baby starts solids, water earns a place at the table:

- **Offer it in an open cup or straw cup**, not a bottle. Bottles of water get drunk in volume; cups get sipped. Our [bottle-to-cup weaning guide](/blog/weaning-bottle-to-cup/) covers cup types.
- **Put it with the meal.** Water alongside food helps with swallowing dry textures and builds the habit of drinking at mealtimes.
- **Expect mess and theatre.** Most of the first few weeks of cup-drinking ends up on the tray. That's fine; it's practice.
- **Don't let it replace milk.** If your baby starts drinking a lot of water and taking noticeably less milk, scale the water back and mention it at the next checkup.
- **Plain water only.** No juice, no sweetened drinks, no honey added to anything before 12 months.

Our [starting solids guide](/blog/starting-solids-six-months/) sets out the wider picture of what changes at six months.

## Hot Weather, Illness and Other Edge Cases

**Hot weather, under 6 months:** the answer is more frequent breast or formula feeds, not water. Babies regulate by feeding more often. Keep them cool and out of direct sun — our [baby sun safety guide](/blog/baby-sun-safety/) covers shade and clothing — and watch nappy output.

**Fever or illness:** don't improvise fluids. If a baby is febrile, vomiting or having diarrhea, ask your pediatrician what to give and how much. Oral rehydration solutions exist precisely because plain water is the wrong tool for replacing what's lost. Our guide to [baby's first fever](/blog/baby-first-fever-when-to-worry/) covers when to call.

**Constipation:** it's a common reason parents reach for water. In a baby under six months, constipation is a reason to speak to a clinician rather than to add water — see [baby constipation remedies by age](/blog/baby-constipation-remedies-by-age/) for how it's usually approached.

**Well water or tap water:** boiling and cooling tap water for babies under six months is standard advice in many places if you're making formula; well water has separate considerations, including nitrate levels, worth checking with your local health department.

> ⚠️ **Call your pediatrician promptly if** your baby under six months has had more than occasional small amounts of water and seems unusually sleepy, irritable, floppy, cool to touch, or has any twitching or seizure activity — these can be signs of low blood sodium and need urgent assessment. Also call for fewer wet diapers than usual, a sunken soft spot, no tears when crying, a dry mouth, or unusual lethargy, which can signal dehydration. Both ends of the fluid spectrum are reasons to ring rather than to adjust drinks yourself.

## Common Questions About Babies and Water

### Can newborns have water?

No, not routinely. For roughly the first six months, breast milk or formula provides all the fluid a healthy baby needs. Giving plain water carries two risks: it displaces the milk that supplies their calories and nutrients, and because infant kidneys are still immature, a significant volume can dilute blood sodium — a condition known as water intoxication. If a clinician advises water for a specific reason, follow that; otherwise skip it.

### How much water can a 6-month-old have?

Small amounts, sipped from a cup with meals, rather than a bottle to drink between feeds. CDC infant nutrition guidance introduces plain water alongside solids from around six months, and a few ounces a day is the sort of volume commonly described. Breast milk or formula remains the main drink to twelve months. If water starts noticeably reducing milk intake, cut back and raise it at your next checkup.

### Does my baby need extra water in hot weather?

Under six months, the recommended approach is more frequent breast or formula feeds rather than water — babies hydrate by feeding more often, and milk is mostly water. Keep them cool, shaded and lightly dressed, and watch nappy output as your gauge. Over six months, offering water with meals and a little more often on hot days is reasonable. If you're worried a baby of any age isn't getting enough fluid, call your pediatrician.

### What is water intoxication in babies?

It's a dilution of sodium in the blood — hyponatremia — that can happen when a baby takes in more plain water than their immature kidneys can clear, including from over-diluted formula. Reported signs include unusual irritability or drowsiness, low body temperature, puffiness, and in severe cases seizures. It's uncommon and preventable, which is why guidance against routine water before six months is so consistent. Any of those signs warrants urgent medical assessment.

### Should I give my baby water for constipation?

Not on your own initiative, particularly under six months. Infant constipation has several possible causes and the approach differs by age and by whether a baby is breastfed, formula-fed or on solids — so it's a question for your pediatrician rather than a water problem to solve at home. After six months, water and certain fruits are often part of the advice, but it's still worth getting the plan from a clinician who knows your baby.

## The Bottom Line

- **Under 6 months: no routine water** — breast milk or formula covers all fluid needs, hot weather included
- **From ~6 months: small sips with meals**, in an open or straw cup, with milk still the main drink
- **From 12 months: water becomes a main drink** alongside whole cow's milk
- **Dilution is the real risk**, not fussiness — infant kidneys can't clear excess water, and over-diluted formula carries the same risk
- **Illness and dehydration need a clinician's plan**, not improvised plain water
- **Unusual sleepiness, floppiness, coolness or twitching after water** is an urgent call

The park advice comes from a good place. The better version of it on a hot day is simply: feed more often, find shade, and keep the water for when your baby is old enough to sip it at the table.
