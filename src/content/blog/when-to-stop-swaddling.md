Swaddling is the one newborn trick that feels like cheating. You wrap them up, the flailing arms stop waking them, and you get a stretch of sleep. Which makes the next bit genuinely unwelcome news: there's a hard stop, it arrives earlier than most parents want, and it isn't negotiable. The good news is that the transition is usually easier than the dread suggests — a handful of rough nights, not weeks. Here's when to stop, how to do it, and what to use instead.

> 📌 **Key Takeaway:** Stop swaddling when your baby shows any sign of rolling over — that's the non-negotiable line, because a swaddled baby who rolls onto their stomach can't use their arms to lift or turn their head. AAP safe-sleep guidance is clear that swaddling must stop once a baby starts trying to roll, and many babies show those first signs somewhere around 2 to 4 months. A sleeveless sleep sack is the usual replacement: it keeps the warmth and the bedtime cue while leaving arms free. If you're unsure whether your baby is close to rolling, that uncertainty is itself a reason to transition now. This is general information — your pediatrician is the right person to ask about your baby.

## The One Sign That Means Tonight

Not a date on the calendar. A behavior.

Any of these means the swaddle comes off:

- **Rolling from back to side or to front** — even once, even clumsily
- **Rolling attempts:** lifting a shoulder and hip, rocking side to side, getting onto one side and staying there
- **Working out of the swaddle** regularly, so an arm is loose and the fabric is loosening around the chest and face
- **Pushing up strongly in tummy time**, which usually precedes rolling by a short margin

The mechanism is what makes this firm. A baby who rolls to their stomach with arms pinned has no way to reposition or lift their head clear. Rolling is also a skill babies practise *in their sleep*, often before you've seen it in the day — which is why "they haven't rolled yet" is weaker evidence than it feels. Our guides to [when babies roll over](/blog/when-do-babies-roll-over/) and [safe sleep and SIDS prevention](/blog/baby-sleep-safety-sids-prevention/) cover the broader rules.

The rolling-sign situation changes which of these is available to you. If your baby is *already* showing rolling signs, the gradual methods aren't a safe luxury — go straight to arms-out. The staged approaches are for parents getting ahead of it before the signs appear.

My honest take after doing both: the gradual method mostly extends the number of mediocre nights. Babies adapt to the new normal faster when it's just the new normal.

## What to Use Instead

**A sleeveless sleep sack** (also sold as a wearable blanket) is the standard replacement. It keeps a baby warm without loose bedding in the crib, which is why it sits comfortably within safe-sleep guidance, and it preserves the bedtime cue of being zipped into something. Points worth knowing:

- **Sleeveless, not weighted.** Weighted sleep sacks and weighted swaddles are not recommended for infants; skip them.
- **Right size.** The neck opening should be snug enough that the sack can't ride up over the face, and the body roomy enough for hips and legs to move freely.
- **Appropriate warmth.** Sacks are sold with TOG ratings; dress underneath according to room temperature. Overheating is a safe-sleep concern in its own right.
- **Nothing else in the crib.** Firm mattress, fitted sheet, baby on their back, no blankets, bumpers, pillows or positioners. Our [perfect sleep environment guide](/blog/baby-sleep-environment/) has the full setup.
- **Transitional products exist** — sacks with removable or convertible arm sections — and they're fine, as long as arms end up genuinely free once rolling starts.

## Surviving the First Few Nights

The startle reflex is the real reason the swaddle worked, and it doesn't vanish on schedule. Expect some arm-flinging wake-ups for a few nights. What helps:

- **Keep everything else identical.** Same bath, same feed, same room, same white noise, same order. Change one variable only.
- **Start with naps or start with night — pick one and commit.** Switching back and forth between swaddled naps and unswaddled nights drags it out.
- **Give a minute before intervening.** A startled baby who's resettling sounds alarming and often puts themselves back down. Charging in resets the process.
- **Expect three nights, not three weeks.** If you're still in it after a week with no improvement, something else may be going on — a [sleep regression](/blog/four-month-sleep-regression/), teething, or hunger. Our [4-month sleep regression guide](/blog/four-month-sleep-regression/) covers the overlap, which is real: the swaddle transition and the 4-month regression often arrive together, which is thoroughly unfair.
- **Plenty of daytime tummy time.** Babies who've practised lifting and pushing manage the rolling stage better.

> ⚠️ **Stop swaddling immediately and ask your pediatrician if:** your baby has rolled or is attempting to roll while swaddled; you find them face-down; the swaddle has come loose near the face; or they're breathing noisily or sweating in the wrap. Also raise it with your pediatrician if your baby's hips were ever a concern — swaddling needs to leave legs able to bend up and out, and hip-related questions deserve a clinician's answer rather than an article's.

## A Note on Swaddling Safely Before You Stop

While you're still swaddling, the basics bear repeating because they're what the safety of the practice rests on: snug across the arms and chest but loose around the hips and legs, nothing over the face, always placed on the back, and never swaddled in a bed with an adult or on a sofa. Safe-sleep guidance from the AAP and the CDC covers the full picture; the swaddle is only safe inside those other rules, never as a substitute for them.

## Common Questions About Stopping Swaddling

### At what age should you stop swaddling?

There isn't a single age — the trigger is rolling, not a birthday. AAP safe-sleep guidance says swaddling should stop once a baby starts trying to roll, and many babies show the first signs somewhere between 2 and 4 months. Some are earlier and some later. If you're unsure whether your baby is close to rolling, treat that uncertainty as a reason to transition now rather than to wait for confirmation.

### Is it dangerous to swaddle a baby who can roll?

Yes, which is why the guidance is firm. A swaddled baby who rolls onto their stomach can't use their arms to push up, turn their head or reposition, so swaddling past the rolling stage raises the risk of an unsafe sleep position. Babies also practise rolling during sleep, often before parents have seen it in the daytime, so the safest approach is to stop at the first signs rather than the first confirmed roll.

### How do I transition from swaddle to sleep sack?

Three approaches work: one arm out for a couple of nights then both, keeping the familiar wrap with both arms free, or swapping straight to a sleeveless sleep sack. If rolling signs have already appeared, go straight to arms free. Keep everything else about the routine identical, commit to the change for both naps and nights rather than alternating, and expect roughly one to three harder nights.

### Will my baby sleep worse without the swaddle?

Usually a little, briefly. The startle reflex is why the swaddle helped and it doesn't disappear on cue, so a few nights of arm-flinging wake-ups are normal. Most babies settle into the new setup within about three nights. Giving a startled baby a minute to resettle before going in helps, as does plenty of daytime tummy time. If poor sleep persists beyond a week, consider teething, hunger or a regression rather than the sack.

### Are weighted sleep sacks safe for babies?

Weighted sleep sacks and weighted swaddles are not recommended for infants, and safe-sleep guidance steers away from anything that adds weight or restricts movement on a sleeping baby. A plain sleeveless sleep sack with an appropriate TOG rating for the room is the standard option. If you've been given a weighted product as a gift, your pediatrician is the right person to ask before using it.

## The Bottom Line

- **Rolling — or trying to roll — is the stop sign**, not an age
- **Many babies hit that point between 2 and 4 months**, some sooner
- **A sleeveless sleep sack is the standard replacement** — warmth and routine cue, arms free
- **Skip weighted sacks and swaddles** for infants
- **Change one variable only** and commit for naps and nights alike
- **Expect about three rough nights**, and check for teething or a regression if it drags on past a week

If you're reading this at midnight having just watched your swaddled baby heave onto one side: tonight's the night. Get the sack out, keep everything else the same, and take comfort that this is one of the shorter parenting transitions.
