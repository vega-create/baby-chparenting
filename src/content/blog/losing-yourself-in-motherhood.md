A friend asked me, about eight months into my first baby's life, what I'd been reading lately. I opened my mouth and nothing came out. Not because I couldn't remember a book; because I couldn't remember being a person who read books. I had opinions, a job I liked, a playlist, a group chat where I was the funny one. Somewhere between the first feeding and the eight-hundredth, all of that had gone quiet, and I hadn't noticed until someone asked. If you're reading this at 2 a.m. with a baby on your chest, wondering where you went, this is for you.

> 📌 **Key Takeaway:** Losing yourself in early motherhood is a near-universal experience, and there's even a word for the transition: matrescence, the identity shift of becoming a mother, as real as adolescence. The feeling isn't a sign you're doing it wrong; it's a sign you're doing something enormous. You don't get the old self back unchanged, but you can rebuild a version of yourself that includes the mother and the person: one small thread at a time, with as little as ten minutes a day. If the lost feeling comes with persistent sadness, numbness, or hopelessness, that's worth a conversation with your doctor.

## Why it feels like you've disappeared

Several things happen at once in the first year, and together they can erase the outline of who you were:

- **Your time stops being yours.** Every hour is scheduled by someone else's hunger and sleep. The things that made you *you* (reading, running, friends, work, a long shower) were all things you did in time you controlled, and that time is gone.
- **Your body changes and keeps changing.** Pregnancy, birth, breastfeeding, sleep deprivation. It's hard to feel like yourself in a body that doesn't feel like yours. The [postpartum body image](/blog/postpartum-body-image/) article goes further on this.
- **Your relationships shift.** Friends without kids drift. Your partner becomes a co-worker in a 24-hour operation. Conversations shrink to logistics.
- **Your name changes.** You're "Mama," "Liam's mom," "the mom" at the pediatrician's office. Nobody at daycare drop-off knows what you do or did or think.
- **Your brain is busy.** Sleep loss and the constant background task of monitoring a small human leave very little working memory for the parts of you that aren't about the baby.

## Matrescence: this has a name

The anthropologist Dana Raphael coined the term "matrescence" in the 1970s to describe the process of becoming a mother, and it has been picked up more recently by researchers and clinicians because it captures something the word "postpartum" doesn't. Like adolescence, it's a developmental transition: hormonal, physical, psychological, and social all at once. Like adolescence, it's disorienting. And like adolescence, you come out the other side as someone new, not as the person you were before with a baby attached.

Naming it matters because it reframes the loss. You haven't failed to hold onto yourself. You're in the middle of becoming a different self, and that's supposed to feel strange.

## The guilt that keeps you stuck

Most moms I know feel two things at once: grief for the person they were, and guilt for grieving when they have a healthy baby they love. The guilt is what keeps the grief from moving. So let's be clear: wanting your own life back is not the same as not wanting your child. The two coexist in almost every mother, and pretending the first one doesn't exist just makes it louder.

## Finding your way back: threads, not transformations

You're not going to get a weekend retreat. You might get ten minutes. The strategy is to pull small threads back into your days until, months from now, the fabric looks like you again.

### 1. Reclaim one old thing, in miniature

Pick one thing that was yours before: a podcast, a sport, a craft, a type of book, a cuisine. Do it in its smallest possible form. Ten pages. One song played loud. A fifteen-minute walk that's a run in your head. The point is not the activity. The point is proving the person who liked it still exists.

### 2. Find the minutes

They exist, but they're hidden inside naps, early mornings, and the hour after bedtime you currently spend scrolling. The [me-time calculator](/tools/me-time-calculator/) shows you where your day actually goes and where fifteen minutes can come from. If naps are chaos, the [bedtime calculator](/tools/bedtime-calculator/) and our [nap guide](/blog/how-many-naps-by-age/) can help make them predictable.

### 3. Talk to adults as yourself, not as a mom

Have one conversation a week that isn't about the baby. Tell your partner about something you read or saw. Call the friend who never asks about sleep schedules. Join a book club, a class, a volunteer thing, even online. Being seen as a person by other people is the fastest way to remember you are one.

### 4. Keep a one-line record

Each night, one sentence in the [mood journal](/tools/mood-journal/): how you felt, one thing that was yours today. In a month you'll have a record that the person is still in there, showing up in small ways. It's surprisingly powerful to read back.

### 5. Say something true about yourself every day

The [daily affirmation](/tools/daily-affirmation-for-moms/) is deliberately not about being a great mom. It's about being a whole person. "I'm allowed to want things." "My rest is not a reward." One sentence a day is a small, stubborn act of self-recognition.

### 6. Let the new self include the mother

The goal isn't to subtract motherhood and get the old you back. It's to build a self where the mother and the person both have room. Some things you loved won't come back, and new things will take their place. The woman who used to spend Saturday mornings at the climbing gym might become the one who takes her kid hiking and discovers she likes that more. That's not loss. That's matrescence finishing its work.

Two threads that other moms have picked up: if faith is part of your life, our sister site Faith Notes for Moms collects [Bible verses about motherhood](https://faith.chparenting.com/blog/bible-verses-about-motherhood/) written for the week when nothing is left; and if having something that earns a little and is entirely yours appeals, [Startup Notes for Moms](https://startup.chparenting.com/blog/best-side-hustles-for-stay-at-home-moms-2026/) ranks side hustles by what the author has actually done, not what sounds good.

## A 30-day thread plan

If you want somewhere to start, here's a month that asks almost nothing of you:

- **Week 1:** Each night, write one line: one thing today that was yours. That's it. Most moms find three or four by the end of the week that they hadn't noticed.
- **Week 2:** Add ten minutes a day of one old thing in miniature. Same thing every day; don't choose fresh each time.
- **Week 3:** Add one adult conversation that isn't about the baby. Schedule it like an appointment.
- **Week 4:** Add one hour, once, out of the house, alone. Then read back the journal.

Four small threads in thirty days. Not a transformation, but by the end you'll have evidence, in your own handwriting, that the person is still there.

## Why your partner needs to hear this

If you have a partner, say the words: "I feel like I've disappeared, and I need some time each week to be a person who isn't a mother." Partners often don't notice the erasure because, from the outside, you look busy and competent. Make the ask specific (Saturday mornings, Tuesday evenings) and make it recurring. One-off breaks don't rebuild an identity. A rhythm does.

## When it's more than identity loss

Feeling lost is normal. Feeling nothing is different. Talk with your doctor, OB-GYN, or your baby's pediatrician if, for more than two weeks, you notice:

- Persistent sadness, emptiness, or hopelessness
- Numbness or feeling disconnected from your baby
- No interest in anything, including the things you're trying to reclaim
- Constant anxiety, racing thoughts, or panic
- Sleep problems even when the baby sleeps
- Thoughts that your family would be better off without you

Perinatal depression and anxiety are common, they often masquerade as "just losing myself," and they respond to treatment. In the US, call or text **988** any time, or call the **National Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262** (1-833-TLC-MAMA). **Postpartum Support International** runs free online support groups where "I don't know who I am anymore" is one of the most common sentences spoken.

## The Bottom Line

You didn't lose yourself by accident or by failing. You walked into one of the biggest identity transitions a human goes through, in a body that's changing, on no sleep, with your time handed to someone else. The way back isn't a grand gesture. It's one old thing in miniature, one adult conversation a week, one true sentence a day, and a partner or friend who knows what you're trying to do. Months from now, the person looking back from the mirror will be new, and she'll be yours.
