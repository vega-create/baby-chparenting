My daughter's worst tantrums happened at the exact moment I had the least left: 5:30 p.m., dinner half-made, baby fussing, her shoes "wrong." I'd try to reason with her, which is like trying to reason with a fire alarm, and by the end we'd both be crying. What eventually worked wasn't a better discipline strategy. It was realizing that a toddler can't calm down alone, and that the activities that settle her nervous system settle mine too. These six are the ones we still use. They take two to ten minutes, need nothing you don't already own, and work on both of you at once.

> 📌 **Key Takeaway:** Toddlers don't have the brain wiring yet to calm themselves; they borrow calm from an adult, a process called co-regulation. That means the most effective calm-down activities are ones you do *with* them, and the physical ones (slow breathing, deep pressure, rhythmic movement) regulate the parent's nervous system at the same time. Use them when the meltdown is winding down, not at its peak, and practice them on calm days so they're familiar when the storm hits. For the tantrum itself, safety and quiet presence come first; teaching comes after.

## Why you can't calm a toddler by telling them to calm down

The part of the brain that manages big feelings isn't developed in a two-year-old; pediatric guidance on tantrums describes them as a normal part of development precisely because toddlers have strong emotions and few tools. What a toddler can do is sync up with a calm adult body: slower breathing, lower voice, steady touch. That's co-regulation, and it's the reason "calm down" never works but a slow, warm "I'm here" sometimes does.

The catch: you have to actually be calm, or at least calmer. Which is why the best activities on this list are the ones that regulate you first. Our guides to [toddler tantrums by age](/blog/toddler-tantrums-by-age/) and [biting and hitting](/blog/toddler-biting-and-hitting/) cover what's normal at each stage; this article is about what to do with your bodies.

## 1. Bubble breathing (ages 1.5 and up)

Hold up a bubble wand, real or imaginary, and ask your toddler to blow the biggest bubble they can. A big bubble needs a long, slow exhale, which is exactly the breath that calms the nervous system. Do it with them: "Let's blow a huge one together." Five or six bubbles and you'll both notice your shoulders drop. Real bubbles work best for the first few times; after that, pretend ones work in the car, the grocery line, anywhere. If you want to practice the grown-up version alone, the [breathing exercise](/tools/breathing-exercise/) uses the same long-exhale rhythm.

## 2. The squeeze game (ages 1 and up)

Firm, steady pressure is calming for most small children (and most adults). Sit behind your toddler and give slow, firm squeezes down their arms and legs, "squeezing the wiggles out," or wrap them in a blanket "like a burrito" and hold them. Ask: "Big squeeze or little squeeze?" and let them choose. The pressure settles their sensory system; the holding settles yours. Many kids who hate being touched mid-tantrum will accept this once the peak has passed.

## 3. The calm-down corner (ages 2 and up)

Not a time-out spot. A cozy spot: a cushion, a soft blanket, a couple of board books, a stuffed animal, maybe a small bottle of glitter and water to shake and watch settle. Introduce it on a good day as "the cozy corner where we go when our feelings are too big." The key is that you go there too, at least at first. Sit on the floor with them. The corner teaches them that big feelings have a place to go, and it gives you a place to sit down, which you probably needed.

## 4. Rock and hum (all ages)

Pick them up (or sit with them in your lap) and rock slowly while humming a low, steady note or a simple tune. Rhythm and low-frequency sound are soothing for small children, and humming lengthens your own exhale without you having to think about it. This is the one for the overtired, no-words, end-of-the-day meltdown, and it works on a fussy baby just as well as on a toddler. If background sound helps, the [white noise player](/tools/white-noise/) can run underneath.

## 5. Animal walks (ages 2 and up)

When the energy is high and angry rather than sad: "Let's stomp like elephants to the kitchen." "Can you crawl like a bear to the bathroom?" "Hop like a frog to your shoes." Heavy, whole-body movement burns off the surge of stress chemistry in a toddler's body, and doing it alongside them (yes, you stomp too) does the same for you. Bonus: it gets the shoes on.

## 6. Name it together (ages 2.5 and up)

Once the storm has passed, help them put a word on it: "You were so mad about the shoes. Mad is a big feeling." Keep it to one or two sentences. Naming feelings is a skill toddlers build slowly, and hearing you name your own helps: "I felt frustrated too. I took some big breaths." You're modeling the whole sequence: feeling, naming, calming, moving on. The [mood journal](/tools/mood-journal/) on this site is the grown-up version of the same practice.

## What to keep in a calm-down kit

A small basket, kept where the meltdowns usually happen (for us, the kitchen), means the tools are in reach when nobody can think:

- A bubble wand (the cheap kind, plus a spare)
- A soft blanket for the burrito squeeze
- A sensory bottle: a clear water bottle with water, a squeeze of clear glue, and glitter, lid glued shut. Shake it and watch the glitter settle while you breathe.
- Two or three board books about feelings
- A small stuffed animal that "needs" calming ("Can you show Bear how to blow bubbles?")
- For you: a glass of water and a sticky note with one sentence, like "Low and slow."

Nothing in it costs more than a few dollars, and the basket itself becomes a cue: when it comes out, both of you know what happens next.

## During the tantrum itself

The activities above are for the wind-down. At the peak, less is more:

- **Safety first.** Move them away from hard edges or the sibling. Hold them if they're hurting themselves.
- **Stay close and quiet.** A calm presence beats words. "I'm here" is enough.
- **Don't negotiate or explain.** They can't hear it yet.
- **Keep your voice low and slow,** even if you have to fake it. Your tone is the thing they'll sync to.
- **Wait for the shift.** The sobbing changes pitch, the body softens. That's when bubble breathing or the squeeze game can start.

If you feel your own anger spiking mid-tantrum, that's the moment to put the toddler somewhere safe and take six breaths in the next room. The [mom rage article](/blog/mom-rage-why-it-happens-and-what-helps/) covers what to do when you're the one melting down.

## Practice on the good days

A calm-down tool introduced during a meltdown is just one more demand. Introduced on a calm Tuesday as a game, it becomes familiar, and familiar is what a dysregulated toddler can reach for. Blow bubbles after lunch. Do animal walks to the bath. Visit the cozy corner to read. By the time the storm hits, the tools are already theirs, and yours.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Tantrums are normal through the toddler years. Talk with your pediatrician if they're very frequent or very long (regularly more than 15 to 20 minutes), if your child is hurting themselves or others routinely, if meltdowns are getting more intense after age four, or if you're worried about your own ability to stay safe during them. And if the tantrums have become the thing that tips you into persistent hopelessness, rage, or anxiety, that's a reason to talk with your own doctor as well. In the US, the **988 Lifeline** (call or text 988) and the **National Maternal Mental Health Hotline (1-833-852-6262)** are there for the parent side.

## The Bottom Line

A toddler can't calm down alone, and you can't calm a toddler from a state of panic. The activities that work are the ones that settle both bodies at once: long exhales disguised as bubbles, firm squeezes, a cozy corner you sit in too, rocking and humming, heavy animal walks, and naming the feeling once it's passed. Practice them on calm days, use them when the storm is winding down, and let them do double duty. The calm you lend your child is calm you get to keep.
