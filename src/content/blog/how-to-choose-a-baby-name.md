Every baby-name article in the world is a list. Lists are fun, and they are exactly why some couples arrive at 38 weeks with 60 candidates and no decision. The problem was never a shortage of names; it was the absence of a process for cutting them. What follows is a decision framework rather than another list — seven tests to run a shortlist through, a sane way to use popularity data, and a method for the conversation where you and your partner want different things.

> 📌 **Key Takeaway:** Name regret almost never comes from choosing an unpopular name or an unusual spelling. It comes from choosing under pressure, choosing to please someone else, or choosing a name you hadn't actually said out loud in real life. Use the Social Security Administration's own data rather than trend articles — the SSA publishes the 1,000 most popular US names each year around Mother's Day, searchable back to 1880 and by state, drawn from Social Security card applications. Then run your shortlist through seven practical tests before you commit, and remember that in the US you have a window after birth to register the name, so nobody has to decide in a delivery room.

## First, Build the Shortlist Properly

Two rules make everything downstream easier.

**Rule one: separate generating from judging.** Spend one session adding names with no vetoes allowed — browse by [meaning](/blog/names-meaning-strength/), by heritage, by sound, by family tree. Then stop. Judge in a different session, on a different day. Mixing the two is why name conversations end badly: one person is still brainstorming while the other has started eliminating.

**Rule two: cap it at ten.** A shortlist of thirty isn't a shortlist. If a name doesn't survive a week in your head, it goes.

Notice what isn't on the list: whether anyone else likes it. That's deliberate — see below.

## Using Popularity Data Instead of Trend Articles

Trend pieces are written in advance and often guess. The SSA's own data is the real thing: it publishes the 1,000 most popular names each year around Mother's Day, built from Social Security card applications at birth, and lets you search popularity by year back to 1880 and by state.

The most recent release, in May 2026, covered names given in 2025. Olivia and Liam topped the lists for the seventh year running, Charlotte moved up to second among girls, ending Emma's six-year run as runner-up, Ava dropped out of the girls' top ten, and Eliana entered at number ten.

Two ways to actually use this:

- **If you want to avoid duplicates in a classroom**, check the state-level list, not the national one. Regional distribution varies a lot, and "popular nationally" and "three in your daughter's class" are different questions.
- **If you want a name that feels familiar but not ubiquitous**, look at ranks roughly 100–400. Recognizable, spellable, rarely repeated. Our guides to [vintage names making a comeback](/blog/vintage-baby-names-comeback/) and [unique and rare names](/blog/unique-rare-baby-names/) sit on either side of that band.

## When You and Your Partner Disagree

The mechanics that work better than arguing:

1. **Separate lists, written privately.** No peeking, no lobbying.
2. **Swap and mark only two categories:** "could live with" and "absolute no." Three or more categories turn into negotiation.
3. **Work only from the overlap of "could live with."** If the overlap is empty, each of you adds five new names and repeat. Don't try to convert a no — vetoes are free and unlimited, and respecting that keeps the process going.
4. **Trade dimensions, not names.** One of you picks the first name, the other the middle; or one names this baby, the other the next. This works far better than splitting a single name down the middle.
5. **Try a two-week test drive.** Use the leading candidate privately for a fortnight — in your head, in texts to each other. Names reveal themselves in use.

Middle names are the great pressure valve here, which is why they're worth taking seriously rather than treating as filler — our [middle name guide](/blog/best-middle-name-ideas/) covers that.

## Managing Everyone Else

Three things to decide in advance:

**Whether to share the name before birth.** Sharing invites commentary; not sharing invites "but we need to know!" The middle path that works: share the shortlist with no one, and announce the chosen name with the birth, when it's a fact rather than a proposal.

**How to respond to a bad reaction.** One sentence, no defense: "We love it." Explaining invites debate; a flat statement ends it. A relative's face at the announcement is not new information about the name.

**Family expectation.** If there's pressure to use a particular family name and you don't want it as a first name, the middle slot resolves most of this. If it doesn't, a name you resent for forty years is a worse outcome than a disappointed relative for forty days.

## About Name Regret

The honest version: it's common, usually mild, and usually temporary. A new name on a new person feels strange for a few weeks no matter how carefully chosen — that strangeness is unfamiliarity, not error.

Where it turns into real regret is predictable. It comes from choosing to please someone else, choosing under time pressure in a hospital, picking a spelling you weren't sure about, or realizing you never once said the name out loud before signing the form. All four are preventable by the process above.

And if you're genuinely unhappy after a few weeks: in the US, name changes for infants are possible, and the process varies by state. It's more administrative than dramatic. But sit with it for a month first — most of the strangeness wears off.

## Common Questions About Choosing a Baby Name

### How do I choose a baby name when we can't agree?

Write separate private lists, swap them, and mark each name only as "could live with" or "absolute no." Work only from the overlap of the first category, and don't try to argue anyone out of a veto. If the overlap is empty, each of you adds five new names and you repeat. Trading dimensions — one picks the first name, the other the middle — resolves more standoffs than negotiating over a single name ever does.

### Where can I find reliable baby name popularity data?

The Social Security Administration publishes the official US data: the 1,000 most popular names each year, released around Mother's Day, drawn from Social Security card applications at birth. You can search popularity by year back to 1880 and by state. State-level lists are more useful than the national top ten if your real question is how many children in your child's class will share the name.

### What were the most popular baby names in the US in 2025?

In the SSA's May 2026 release covering 2025 births, Olivia and Liam ranked first for the seventh consecutive year. The girls' top ten was Olivia, Charlotte, Emma, Amelia, Sophia, Mia, Isabella, Evelyn, Sofia and Eliana; the boys' was Liam, Noah, Oliver, Theodore, Henry, James, Elijah, Mateo, William and Lucas. Charlotte rose to second, Ava left the top ten, and Eliana entered at number ten.

### Should I tell people the name before the baby is born?

Entirely your call, but it's worth deciding deliberately rather than by default. Sharing early invites opinions you then have to manage; announcing with the birth presents the name as a fact rather than a proposal, which tends to end commentary quickly. If you do share and get a negative reaction, a single sentence — "we love it" — closes the conversation better than any explanation.

### Is it normal to regret a baby name?

Mild, temporary doubt is very common. A new name on a new person feels unfamiliar for a few weeks regardless of how much thought went into it. Lasting regret tends to have specific causes: choosing to please someone else, deciding under pressure in the hospital, or never having said the name aloud in daily life. Sit with it for a month before acting — and if it persists, infant name changes are possible in the US, with the process varying by state.

## The Bottom Line

- **Generate and judge in separate sessions**, and cap the shortlist at ten
- **Run the seven tests**: say it aloud, initials, nicknames, spelling, three ages, siblings and surname, search
- **Use SSA data, not trend lists** — and check state-level ranks if classroom duplicates are your worry
- **Ranks 100–400 are the familiar-but-not-everywhere band**
- **Settle disagreements by veto and overlap**, and trade first-versus-middle rather than arguing
- **Announce rather than consult** — and answer criticism with one sentence
- **Early doubt is normal**; real regret comes from choosing under pressure or to please others

You don't need more names. You need a shortlist of five that have survived being said out loud, and the willingness to pick one.
