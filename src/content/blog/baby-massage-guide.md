Baby massage sits in an odd spot. It's genuinely lovely, it costs nothing, and it has a real research literature behind it — and that literature is more modest than the classes sometimes suggest. Here's what reviews actually found, plus a simple routine you can do on a changing mat in five minutes without buying anything. It's also one of the few baby activities where the clearest documented benefit is to the parent.

> 📌 **Key Takeaway:** A systematic review of infant massage reported possible benefits including pain relief, help with jaundice and weight gain, while urging caution in interpreting the results and noting that no adverse effects were observed. The findings for parents are more consistent: reviews report improved maternal wellbeing and reduced anxiety, depression and stress. NHS patient guidance frames it around consent and cues — asking the baby's permission, keeping the routine the same each time, and pausing or stopping if they show stress signals. Massage is a nice thing to do, not a treatment: persistent symptoms like colic, constipation or reflux need a pediatric assessment, and one review advises checking with a clinician before starting if your baby has a health condition. This is general information.

## What the Evidence Says (Honestly)

The strongest research on infant massage comes from neonatal units, with preterm babies, which limits how far it generalizes to a healthy term baby on your living-room floor. Within that literature:

- **For babies:** a systematic review found possible benefits for pain relief, jaundice and weight gain, but the authors explicitly urged caution, and noted that not all differences between groups reached statistical significance. No adverse effects were observed.
- **For parents:** this is where it's most consistent. Reviews report improvements in mothers' mental wellbeing and reductions in anxiety, depression and stress, and one study of fathers found lower stress after a massage class.
- **For sleep:** NHS patient information describes massage as linked to improved sleep patterns for infants and their parents — worth noting that this is patient-information guidance rather than trial evidence.

So the fair summary: do it because it's a calm, pleasant, connecting part of the day with no known downsides, and because the parent half of the dyad measurably benefits. Don't do it expecting it to resolve a medical problem.

## Before You Start

**Timing matters more than technique.** Choose a moment when your baby is in a quiet, alert state — not hungry, not just fed (wait 30 minutes or so to avoid spit-up), not already overtired. For a lot of families this is after the bath, which is why it slots neatly into a [bedtime routine](/blog/baby-sleep-environment/).

**Set up:** warm room, soft surface, a towel underneath, phone out of reach, your hands warm and jewellery off. Nothing else is needed.

**Ask permission.** This sounds precious and isn't. NHS neonatal guidance suggests asking your infant's permission before beginning, and starting the routine in the same way each time so the baby learns what to expect. In practice: cup your hands on their chest or legs, say the same short phrase every time, and watch their response. A baby who settles and makes eye contact is saying yes. A baby who stiffens, turns away or fusses is saying not now — and respecting that is the whole point, because it's how massage becomes a conversation rather than something done to them.

Five to ten minutes is plenty. One review article suggested roughly 15 minutes a session, a couple of times a week — treat that as a loose guide from a lower-quality source rather than a prescription. Two minutes of legs on a day when that's all anyone can manage still counts.

**Pressure:** firmer than you'd instinctively use for a face, gentler than you'd use on your own calf. Slow is more important than strong; babies respond to rhythm.

## Oils: What to Use and What to Skip

Massage works perfectly well with no oil at all, which is the simplest answer when a baby's skin is unpredictable. If you do use something:

- **Plain, unscented, and as few ingredients as possible.** Avoid essential oils and fragranced products on young babies.
- **Patch test first** on a small area, and wait a day.
- **Ask your pediatrician** before using anything oil-based if your baby has [eczema](/blog/baby-eczema-triggers-treatment/) or broken skin, or if they're very young — skin advice for babies with eczema is specific and worth getting individually.
- **Nut-based oils** are worth a conversation with your clinician if there's family history of allergy.
- **Keep oil off hands and feet** if your baby is anywhere near mouthing them, and remember oily babies are slippery — hold firmly on the way to the cot.

## Reading Their Cues

This is the skill, not the strokes. NHS neonatal guidance is direct about it: if an infant begins to display stress signals during the massage, it's important to pause, slow down or stop.

**Signals to continue:** relaxed limbs, soft gaze or settled eye contact, quiet breathing, small contented sounds.

**Signals to pause or stop:** turning the head away, stiffening or arching, splaying fingers, hiccups, yawning, skin color changes, grimacing, crying.

Yawning and hiccups surprise people — they can be signs of being overwhelmed rather than tired. The general rule: if in doubt, stop earlier. A three-minute massage that ends while everyone's still enjoying it teaches a better association than a ten-minute one that ends in tears.

> ⚠️ **Ask your pediatrician before starting, or stop and seek advice, if:** your baby was born prematurely or has a medical condition — one review specifically advises understanding a child's health condition and consulting a clinician or pediatric massage therapist first; there's a fracture, hip concern, recent surgery, or an unhealed cord stump; your baby has significant eczema or broken skin; or your baby consistently becomes distressed. And a boundary worth naming: ongoing colic, constipation, reflux or poor weight gain are reasons to see a clinician, not problems to massage away. ## Common Questions About Baby Massage

### When can you start baby massage?

Many families begin in the newborn weeks with gentle, short sessions, choosing a calm, alert moment and avoiding the area around an unhealed cord stump. If your baby was born prematurely or has a health condition, ask your pediatrician first — the strongest research base is from neonatal units where massage is done with clinical guidance. Start with two or three minutes on the legs and build up based on how your baby responds.

### Does baby massage actually help with colic or constipation?

A systematic review found possible benefits in some areas while urging caution about the strength of the evidence, and studies on massage for colic and constipation specifically tend to be small. The reasonable position is that gentle clockwise tummy massage may be soothing and is unlikely to hurt, but persistent colic, constipation or reflux warrants a pediatric assessment rather than being managed with massage alone.

### What oil should I use for baby massage?

You don't need any — massage works fine without oil. If you use one, choose plain and unscented with few ingredients, avoid essential oils and fragranced products on young babies, and patch test on a small area first. Ask your pediatrician before using oil if your baby has eczema or broken skin, or if there's a family history of nut allergy and you're considering a nut-based oil. Oily babies are slippery, so hold firmly.

### How long should a baby massage last?

Five to ten minutes suits most babies, and shorter is fine. One review suggested around 15 minutes a couple of times a week, though that comes from a lower-quality source and is best treated as a loose guide. The better measure is your baby's cues: stop while they're still enjoying it rather than pushing to a target length, because ending on a good note is what builds the positive association.

### How do I know if my baby doesn't like being massaged?

Watch for stress signals: turning the head away, stiffening or arching, splayed fingers, hiccups, yawning, grimacing, color changes or crying. NHS guidance advises pausing, slowing down or stopping when these appear. Yawning and hiccups can indicate being overwhelmed rather than tired. Signs to carry on are relaxed limbs, soft eye contact, quiet breathing and contented sounds. If a baby consistently dislikes it, that's information to respect, not a problem to push through.

## The Bottom Line

- **Evidence for babies is promising but modest**; evidence for parents' wellbeing is more consistent
- **Ask permission the same way each time** and treat it as a conversation, not a procedure
- **Legs first, then tummy, chest, arms, face, back** — slow beats firm, and 5–10 minutes is plenty
- **Oil is optional**; plain and unscented if used, and check first for eczema or allergy history
- **Stop at the first stress signals** — turning away, stiffening, hiccups, yawning
- **It's not a treatment** — persistent colic, constipation, reflux or poor weight gain need a clinician

Five minutes, warm hands, no phone. If nothing else, the research is clearest that the person giving the massage feels better afterwards — which, at six weeks postpartum, is reason enough.
