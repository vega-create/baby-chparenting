That first tiny tooth peeking through your baby's gum is a photo-worthy milestone — and the start of a lifetime of dental care. But it also raises practical questions: when should it appear, is your baby "late" if it hasn't, and how do you actually care for a single tooth? This guide covers the typical timeline for that first tooth, the order the rest follow, exactly how to care for baby's new teeth, and when to bring up late teething with your pediatrician or dentist. Grab your camera and let's get you ready.

> 📌 **Key Takeaway:** According to the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry, most babies get their first tooth between 6 and 10 months, though anywhere from 3 to 15 months is normal. Dental care starts with that very first tooth — brush twice daily with a rice-grain smear of fluoride toothpaste, and schedule the first dental visit by the first birthday.

## When Does the First Tooth Appear?

Most babies cut their first tooth between **6 and 10 months**, but the normal range is wide — anywhere from around 3 months to 15 months. A few babies are even born with a tooth, and some don't get their first until after their first birthday. Timing is largely down to genetics, so if you or your partner teethed late, your baby might too.

The first tooth is almost always one of the **bottom two front teeth** (lower central incisors). You might spot it, or feel a hard little bump, before it fully emerges.

By around age 3, most children have their full set of 20 baby teeth. Teeth typically come in pairs, one on each side. For help soothing the discomfort along the way, see our [teething guide](/blog/baby-teething-symptoms-timeline/).

## How to Care for That First Tooth

Dental care begins the moment the first tooth appears — actually, even before. Here's the routine:

- ✅ **Before teeth:** wipe your baby's gums with a soft, damp cloth after feedings
- ✅ **Once the first tooth is in:** brush twice a day with a soft, small infant toothbrush
- ✅ **Use fluoride toothpaste** — a **rice-grain-sized smear** for babies (pea-sized after age 3)
- ✅ **Brush before bed** — this is the most important brushing of the day
- ✅ **Never put baby to bed with a bottle** of milk or juice — pooled sugar causes "baby bottle tooth decay"
- ✅ **Book the first dental visit** by the first birthday, per the AAP and pediatric dentists

> 💡 **On fluoride toothpaste:** Current guidance from pediatric dentists recommends a tiny smear of fluoride toothpaste from the very first tooth (not the older "wait until age 2" advice). The amount is key — just a rice-grain smear — which is safe even if swallowed and protects against cavities from the start.

## Preventing Early Cavities

Baby teeth matter — they hold space for adult teeth and are essential for eating and speech. Protect them:

- ⚠️ **Avoid sugary drinks and juice** in bottles, and never prop a bottle for sleep
- ⚠️ **Don't share spoons or "clean" pacifiers with your mouth** — this can transfer cavity-causing bacteria
- ⚠️ **Limit sweets and sticky foods** as your baby starts solids
- ✅ **Offer water** (for babies over 6 months) rather than juice
- ✅ **Establish the brushing habit early** so it becomes routine

## When Late Teething Is Worth a Mention

Late teething is usually just genetics and nothing to worry about. But mention it at a check-up if:

- Your baby has **no teeth by around 15-18 months**
- There are **other developmental concerns** alongside it
- You notice anything unusual about the gums, jaw, or mouth

For most late teethers, your pediatrician or dentist will simply reassure you. A first tooth arriving "late" doesn't predict anything about your child's future dental health.

## Common Questions About Baby's First Tooth

### At what age do babies get their first tooth?

Most babies get their first tooth between 6 and 10 months, though anywhere from about 3 to 15 months is normal. It's almost always a bottom front tooth. Timing is largely genetic, so a late first tooth often just runs in the family and isn't a cause for concern on its own.

### When should I start brushing my baby's teeth?

Start brushing as soon as the first tooth appears, twice a day, using a soft infant toothbrush and a rice-grain-sized smear of fluoride toothpaste. Before teeth come in, wipe the gums with a damp cloth after feeds. The bedtime brushing is the most important, so never skip it.

### Should I use fluoride toothpaste on my baby?

Yes. Current pediatric dental guidance recommends a rice-grain-sized smear of fluoride toothpaste from the very first tooth (updating the older advice to wait until age 2). This tiny amount is safe even if swallowed and protects against cavities. Increase to a pea-sized amount once your child turns 3.

### When should my baby first see a dentist?

By the first birthday, or within six months of the first tooth appearing — whichever comes first. This early visit lets the dentist check development, spot any issues, and give you personalized care tips. Establishing a "dental home" early sets the foundation for a lifetime of good oral health.

### Is it normal for my 1-year-old to have no teeth?

Often, yes. Some perfectly healthy babies don't get their first tooth until after 12 months. Late teething is usually just genetics. If there are still no teeth by around 15-18 months, mention it at a check-up so your pediatrician or dentist can take a look, but it's rarely a sign of a problem.

## The Bottom Line

That first tooth is a milestone and the start of dental care. Keep these principles in mind:

- **First tooth usually appears at 6-10 months** (wide normal range of 3-15 months)
- **Bottom front teeth come first**, followed by a predictable order
- **Care starts immediately** — brush twice daily with a rice-grain smear of fluoride toothpaste
- **Never bottle-to-bed with milk or juice** — it causes early decay
- **First dental visit by the first birthday**

Snap that photo, then start the twice-daily brushing habit. Baby teeth matter — caring for them well from the very first tooth sets your child up for a healthy smile for years to come.
