On day three, someone — a nurse, your mother, you at 2 a.m. under bad lighting — notices your baby looks a bit yellow. Few things spook a new parent faster, and few things are more common: the majority of newborns develop some visible jaundice in the first week. Most of the time it's a normal, temporary chapter of newborn physiology that resolves on its own. Occasionally it needs treatment, which works extremely well when started on time. The skill for parents is knowing which is which, and when to pick up the phone. Here's the whole picture.

> 📌 **Key Takeaway:** Jaundice — a yellow tint to skin and eyes caused by bilirubin — appears in most newborns around days 2–4 and usually fades within one to two weeks. According to the American Academy of Pediatrics, every newborn should be checked for jaundice before hospital discharge and again in the first days at home, because timing and level determine whether treatment is needed. Jaundice in the first 24 hours of life, deepening yellow after day 5, poor feeding, dark sleepiness, or yellow spreading to arms and legs are reasons to call your pediatrician promptly. This is general education, not medical advice.

## Why Newborns Turn Yellow

Bilirubin is a yellow pigment made when red blood cells are broken down. Everyone produces it; the liver processes it and it leaves the body in stool. Newborns get a triple dose of challenge: they're born with extra red blood cells that break down after birth, their livers are still ramping up, and their intestines are just getting moving — slow stooling means bilirubin gets reabsorbed instead of leaving.

So bilirubin rises in nearly all babies in the first days, becomes visible as jaundice in many, peaks around days 3–5, and then falls as the liver catches up. That's **physiologic jaundice** — the normal kind, and by far the most common.

A few variations worth knowing by name:

- **Breastfeeding (suboptimal intake) jaundice** — in the first week, a baby who isn't yet feeding well has fewer stools, so bilirubin recirculates. The fix is more effective feeding, which is why jaundice checks and [feeding support](/blog/breastfeeding-basics-for-beginners/) go together.
- **Breast milk jaundice** — a different, later pattern: a mild jaundice that lingers for several weeks in some thriving, well-fed breastfed babies. According to the AAP, it's generally harmless and doesn't usually require stopping breastfeeding — but the lingering yellow should still be confirmed as this benign type by your pediatrician rather than assumed.
- **Pathologic causes** — blood type incompatibility between mother and baby, certain enzyme deficiencies (such as G6PD deficiency), infection, or liver problems. These tend to announce themselves differently: jaundice in the first 24 hours, faster rises, or jaundice that won't fade. This is what screening exists to catch.

## How Jaundice Is Checked

You may not have noticed it happening, but your baby was almost certainly screened before you left the hospital. The AAP recommends every newborn be assessed — usually with a painless skin sensor (transcutaneous bilirubinometer) or a small blood sample — and the number interpreted against the baby's age *in hours*, because a level that's fine at 72 hours can be concerning at 24.

At home, check in natural daylight — artificial light lies. Gently press the skin on the nose or chest for a second; the blanched spot shows yellow more clearly if jaundice is present. Yellow typically appears in the face first and moves downward as levels rise, so **yellow reaching the belly, arms or legs suggests a higher level** and deserves a call. In babies with darker skin, skin color is harder to read — the whites of the eyes and the gums are more reliable places to look, and when in doubt, a measurement beats anyone's eyeball.

> ⚠️ **Call your pediatrician promptly if:** jaundice appears in the first 24 hours of life; the yellow is deepening or spreading to arms and legs after day 5; your baby is feeding poorly, unusually sleepy or hard to wake, floppy, or inconsolable with a high-pitched cry; there are fewer wet diapers than expected; stools are pale/chalky or urine is dark; or jaundice is still obvious past two weeks (three in some breastfed babies — but let the doctor make that call). Trust the combination of yellow + not feeding well + very sleepy: that trio is never a "wait and see."

## If Treatment Is Needed

The standard treatment is **phototherapy** — blue-spectrum light that converts bilirubin in the skin into a form the body can excrete without the liver's help. It's been used for decades, it's non-invasive, and it usually brings levels down within a day or two. Depending on the level, it may happen in the hospital under lights, or sometimes at home with a fiber-optic "bili blanket." Babies typically wear just a diaper and eye protection, and feeding continues throughout — feeding is part of the treatment, since stooling carries bilirubin out.

For very high levels, more intensive measures exist (including exchange transfusion in rare cases), which is precisely why screening and follow-up exist: found on time, jaundice almost never gets that far. The AAP's guidance is built around preventing the rare complication of extremely high bilirubin (kernicterus) — and the system of checks works.

Two things that are *not* treatments, despite persistent folklore: indirect sunlight through a window (ineffective at safe exposures, and newborn skin shouldn't be in direct sun at all) and sugar water (slows things down by reducing milk intake). What genuinely helps at home is frequent, effective feeding — which is also why keeping an eye on [diaper output](/blog/newborn-sleep-guide/) is the most useful monitoring a parent can do.

## The Feeding Connection

If there's one practical lever in a parent's hands, it's this: milk in, stool out, bilirubin down. In the first week, aim for at least 8–12 feeds per 24 hours if breastfeeding, waking the baby to feed if needed — very jaundiced babies get sleepy, sleepy babies feed less, and less feeding raises bilirubin further. It's the one loop you can interrupt yourself.

If feeding isn't going well, say so at the jaundice recheck — latch help in week one changes the whole trajectory. Our guides to [getting breastfeeding started](/blog/breastfeeding-basics-for-beginners/) and [how much milk babies need by age](/blog/how-much-milk-by-age/) cover the details, and formula-feeding families can lean on the [formula feeding guide](/blog/formula-feeding-complete-guide/) — jaundice management works the same way: feed well, watch diapers, keep the follow-up appointment.

## Common Questions About Newborn Jaundice

### Is newborn jaundice dangerous?

Usually not. Most newborn jaundice is a normal, temporary process that peaks around days 3–5 and fades within a week or two without treatment. The reason it's taken seriously is that very high bilirubin levels, left untreated, can rarely cause brain injury — which is why the AAP recommends universal screening and early follow-up, and why treatment thresholds are deliberately conservative. Checked on schedule, jaundice is very rarely dangerous.

### When does newborn jaundice go away?

Typically within one to two weeks in formula-fed babies and sometimes up to two or three weeks in breastfed babies, where a mild lingering form called breast milk jaundice can persist in a thriving baby. Jaundice still visible past two weeks should be reviewed by your pediatrician — usually all is well, but persistent jaundice occasionally signals a liver issue that needs testing, particularly if stools are pale.

### How can I tell if my baby's jaundice is getting worse?

Look in natural daylight at the whites of the eyes, the gums, and blanched skin (press gently for a second). Yellow appearing in the face is common; yellow spreading down to the belly, arms and legs suggests a rising level. Worsening feeding, unusual sleepiness, fewer wet diapers, dark urine or pale stools are stronger signals than color alone. When unsure, ask for a measurement — it's quick and settles the question.

### Does breastfeeding cause jaundice?

Two patterns connect them. In week one, a baby who isn't feeding effectively stools less and recirculates bilirubin — the answer is more feeding and lactation support, not stopping. Separately, some well-fed breastfed babies keep a mild jaundice for a few weeks ("breast milk jaundice"), which the AAP describes as generally harmless and rarely a reason to interrupt breastfeeding. Your pediatrician can confirm which pattern applies.

### What is phototherapy and is it safe?

Phototherapy uses blue-spectrum light to convert bilirubin in the skin into a water-soluble form the body can excrete easily. It's non-invasive, has been standard care for decades, and typically lowers levels within 24–48 hours. Babies wear eye protection and continue feeding normally throughout. Side effects are minor — loose stools, mild rash, needing extra fluids — and your care team monitors for them.

## The Bottom Line

- **Most newborns get some jaundice** — it usually appears days 2–4, peaks around days 3–5, and fades within two weeks
- **Timing is the biggest clue:** jaundice in the first 24 hours, or deepening after day 5, is a call-the-doctor pattern
- **Keep the day 3–5 follow-up appointment** — that's when bilirubin peaks and when checks matter most
- **Feeding is your lever:** 8–12 feeds a day, watch wet and dirty diapers, and get latch help early if needed
- **Phototherapy works** and is safe — and sunlight-through-the-window and sugar water are folklore, not treatment

Jaundice sits in that odd category of newborn things that are both extremely common and worth taking seriously — which is exactly what the check-measure-recheck routine is for. Keep the appointments, feed the baby, look at the eyes in daylight, and call when the patterns above show up. Your pediatrician is the person who can assess your actual baby; this article is general information to make those conversations easier.
